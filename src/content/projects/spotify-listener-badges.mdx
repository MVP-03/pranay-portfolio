# Problem

Listening is continuous, but recognition on Spotify is episodic. Wrapped
turns a year of behaviour into a story once, in December, and then the
slate is wiped. In between, a listener can put hundreds of hours into one
artist, catch a future breakout act before anyone else, finish a whole
discography or build a playlist other people live in - and get nothing
persistent back for any of it.

Spotify already measures all of those behaviours. It knows minutes
listened, first-play dates, fan percentiles, album completion, genre and
language spread. The product question isn't "should Spotify add badges?"
It's: which meaningful behaviours does Spotify already measure but fail to
recognise?

The thesis: Spotify shouldn't only tell me what I listened to. It should
show me who I am as a music fan.

## Market evidence

I looked at eight products where badges or status systems affect
contribution, identity, trust or retention - deliberately including the
cases where they didn't work, because a case study that only cites wins
isn't evidence.

TRAKLA2, an online-learning platform, ran a 281-person experiment: total
time on task rose from 11.4h to 13.4h (~+17.5%) and sessions from 10.6 to
12.6 (~+18.9%). Salesforce Trailhead reported 3x monthly Ranger
completions between 2019 and 2021, with progression - not the badge
itself - doing the work. 2025 research on Stack Overflow found badge
design can lift both the quantity and quality of contributions, but that
too much badge variety reduces contribution.

The negative cases matter more. A study of 6,000+ GitHub developers found
most profile achievements had no significant effect on activity. And
research across 1.5M+ Airbnb ratings found Superhost status can *lower*
subsequent ratings - the badge raised expectations faster than it raised
behaviour.

## What the evidence says

Three conclusions shaped the design. Recognition works when the behaviour
it marks already creates value - the badge acknowledges something real
rather than manufacturing it. Progression beats a one-time trophy, which
is why tiers, not trophies, are the unit. And badge quantity is not the
strategy: GitHub and Stack Overflow both show that more achievements can
do nothing or actively hurt. Rarity and meaning are the scarce resources,
not badge count.

## Approach: three systems, not fifty badges

I mapped the full opportunity space - roughly a dozen badge families
spanning fandom, depth, discovery, longevity, album completion, genre and
culture, curation, social and identity - then cut the MVP to three
systems narrow enough to actually test:

**Fandom** - how deeply do I engage with one artist? Built from listening
time, catalogue depth, consistency and percentile: Listener → Dedicated
Fan → Top 10% → Top 1% → Top 0.1%.

**Discovery** - did I find this before it broke? Built from first-listen
timing against defined popularity milestones: Early Listener → Breakout
Witness → First 1,000.

**Listener identity** - what kind of listener am I? Derived archetypes
like Explorer, Loyalist, Archivist, Trendsetter, Album Person, Curator.

Together those three cover status, discovery and self-expression while
keeping the first release small enough to learn from.

## Proof of concept

The POC demonstrates one complete loop rather than the whole ecosystem,
starting with an artist-fandom badge because it's instantly legible,
emotionally relevant, and fully supported by signals Spotify already has.
Five screens: a listener profile that gives recognition a permanent home,
a badge detail view explaining the achievement, a progress view showing
distance to the next tier, an opt-in share card, and a recommended next
milestone that turns recognition into progression.

The behavioural loop it's testing: listen → achieve → reveal → progress →
display → share → discover → listen. The design constraint throughout is
that it should surface meaning that already exists in listening, never
push people toward artificial listening to chase a tier.

## Measurement

I'd run a four-arm experiment - control, badges only, badges plus
progress, badges plus progress plus optional sharing - on a 2-4 week
baseline and a 6-8 week treatment window, comparing cohorts on retention,
listening depth, discovery and artist-relationship metrics.

The north star I'd propose is incremental meaningful listening per
retained user. Notably *not* badges earned: that measures feature
adoption, not value, and optimising it would push the product toward
exactly the badge inflation the evidence warns about.

## Risks and guardrails

The failure modes are predictable, so each gets an explicit guardrail:
badge inflation (keep the core set small, protect rarity), artificial
looping and stream manipulation (meaningful-listening thresholds plus
anti-fraud logic, and never reward behaviour that inflates streams),
expectation inflation à la Airbnb Superhost (describe observable
behaviour precisely, promise no quality guarantee), privacy (sensitive
insights private by default, sharing opt-in), status anxiety (no
mandatory public rankings) and notification fatigue (batched
achievements with user controls).

## Outcome

A complete PM artefact - problem framing, mixed external evidence, badge
opportunity map, scoped MVP, working POC, experiment design, an
11-metric measurement framework, risk register and a five-phase roadmap -
landing on one testable hypothesis: if Spotify recognises meaningful
listening through persistent, progressive, socially legible achievements,
listeners will build a stronger sense of identity and more motivation to
return, explore and go deeper.

The strongest version of the idea isn't a badge gallery. It's a Listener
Identity Layer running across Spotify - "Spotify since 2014, Explorer,
Top 0.5% Radiohead listener, discovered 127 artists before their
breakout, music from 34 countries, 286 albums completed" - which turns
listening history into a living representation of taste, fandom and
discovery.

And the honest next step isn't building fifty badges. It's testing three
behaviours against a control group and learning which forms of
recognition genuinely matter.
